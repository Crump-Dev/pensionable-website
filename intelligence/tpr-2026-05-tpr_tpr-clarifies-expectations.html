TPR clarifies expectations for responsible use of AI in workplace pensions

20 May 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 5 min read

Background: AI adoption is already widespread

The question is no longer whether AI will enter pension scheme operations — it already has. According to the Society of Pension Professionals' 2026 AI Survey, cited in TPR's own AI Plan, adoption among large segments of the industry is now universal, with providers planning deeper integration into core services. Automated member communications, machine-learning fraud detection, transfer calculations, compliance checks, and employer stress monitoring are all in active use across major administrators.

Against that backdrop, TPR published its formal AI Plan on 20 May 2026 — accompanied by press release PN26-12 — setting out, for the first time, a structured regulatory position on how trustees and scheme managers should govern AI use in their schemes. This is not guidance in the traditional sense: more detailed guidance is promised later in 2026 following industry engagement over the summer. What the AI Plan does is establish baseline expectations now, so that schemes cannot claim they were waiting for instruction.

What TPR's AI Plan actually requires

TPR is clear that accountability does not transfer to an algorithm. Trustees and scheme managers remain fully responsible for outcomes even when AI systems are doing the work — and even when those systems are operated by third-party administrators or service providers. This matters practically: a trustee cannot disclaim responsibility for a biased benefit calculation or a mis-sent communication by pointing to software.

The regulator's immediate expectations of trustees and scheme managers are:

  • Establish clear AI governance — including verifying that administrators, advisers and service providers in the supply chain have equally robust arrangements in place.
  • Carry out rigorous testing and ongoing monitoring — both at initial implementation and on a regular basis afterwards.
  • Identify and evaluate risks, maintain appropriate controls, and adapt them as the technology or threat landscape evolves.
  • Protect members from AI-driven fraud — understand how AI is being used by bad actors (clone websites, phishing, impersonation) and respond effectively.
  • Have a clear data strategy — ensure scheme data is high quality, comply with data protection legislation including automated decision-making provisions under UK GDPR.
  • Seek professional advice proportionately when considering or implementing AI innovations.

"Trust is the most valuable asset in our system — and that trust depends on the safe and responsible adoption of AI in members' interests."

Nausicaa Delfas, Chief Executive, The Pensions Regulator — 20 May 2026

Key governance requirements in practice

TPR's principles-based approach — deliberately technology-agnostic to remain relevant as AI evolves — is built around four values: fairness, accountability, transparency, and explainability. For trustees, these translate into concrete operational requirements. Fairness means testing AI outputs for bias that could disadvantage particular cohorts of members, particularly those with protected characteristics. Accountability means maintaining a clear line of human responsibility for every AI-assisted decision. Transparency means being able to explain to a member, in plain terms, what role AI played in a decision affecting them. Explainability means the scheme must understand how the AI model it uses actually works — not simply trust the vendor's assurance.

TPR is also explicit about data quality as a prerequisite for safe AI adoption. AI systems are only as reliable as the data they ingest. Poor data — duplicate records, mismatched NI numbers, missing contribution histories — fed into an AI system produces poor outputs at scale and at speed. The regulator's existing member data guidance applies directly, and trustees are expected to challenge service providers where data standards fall short.

⚠️
The fraud risk is escalating fast

A Lloyds Banking Group survey found nearly 40% of UK adults used AI for financial planning in the past 12 months. TPR warns that AI-generated scams — including fake pension accounts, impersonation fraud, and clone websites — are becoming more sophisticated. Schemes must have active fraud monitoring, not just a policy document.

High-risk vs lower-risk AI uses in pensions

Not all AI applications carry the same governance burden. Routine, lower-stakes uses — such as automated acknowledgement emails, bulk data formatting, or basic compliance flag checks — carry less inherent risk than AI tools making or informing individual member decisions. TPR's AI Plan draws an implicit distinction between administrative productivity uses and decision-support uses that affect members directly.

The highest-risk category is AI being used in or near the advice-guidance boundary: member-facing chatbots that answer questions about retirement options, AI-generated transfer calculations, and tools that generate personalised communications about investment choices. These carry the dual risk of regulatory breach (if they stray into regulated advice) and member harm (if the AI output is wrong or biased). The plan specifically notes the risk of members using unregulated general-purpose AI tools for pension financial planning — 23% of people aged 25–34 trust AI-generated financial insights more than traditional sources, according to the same Lloyds survey — and schemes should factor this into their communications strategies.

What schemes must do now

Detailed guidance from TPR is expected later in 2026. But the AI Plan makes clear that trustees are expected to act immediately, not wait. The regulator's language — "act now" — is unusually direct. Schemes that have not yet mapped where and how AI is being used across their operations and supply chain are already behind expectations.

TPR is also building its own AI capability as part of this agenda. Its AI-enabled scam detection process has already assessed over 2,000 websites and removed 29 high-risk sites, reducing manual triage by around two hours per day. The regulator intends to use AI to identify risks and target enforcement more precisely — meaning AI-governed schemes are likely to attract less scrutiny, while those with poor oversight may find themselves flagged sooner.

Key takeaways for trustees and administrators

  1. Map your AI footprint now. Identify every point in your scheme's operations — including the administrator's and advisers' systems — where AI is being used. You cannot govern what you have not mapped.
  2. Treat data quality as an AI prerequisite. Invest in data remediation before deploying or expanding AI use. Poor input data produces unreliable AI outputs at scale — and trustees remain accountable for those outputs.
  3. Build an audit trail for AI-assisted decisions. For any decision affecting a member — benefit calculations, transfer values, retirement option communications — document what role AI played, who reviewed it, and what controls were in place.
  4. Engage with TPR's summer consultation. The regulator is inviting industry input to shape its forthcoming detailed AI guidance. Trustees and administrators who engage early will have a better chance of influencing a framework that works for their operational context.
