Pension value to be put under the spotlight

08 January 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 5 min read

The value question — why now

TPR's January 2026 announcement that pension value will be "put under the spotlight" marks the point at which value for money assessment shifts from an aspiration into a regulatory obligation. The backdrop is a decade-long consolidation of the DC pensions market, which has driven costs down significantly — but has also raised legitimate questions about whether lower costs translate into better member outcomes, and whether the remaining smaller schemes can genuinely compete with the major providers on net returns.

The joint Value for Money framework — developed collaboratively by TPR, the FCA, and DWP — represents the regulator's response. Where previous value assessments were largely self-assessed and backward-looking, the new framework introduces standardised metrics, comparative benchmarking across the market, and — for schemes that persistently fail to demonstrate value — a clear obligation to consider consolidation. The era of opaque, internally-generated value statements is drawing to a close.

What the framework measures

The VFM framework is structured around three pillars, each designed to capture a distinct dimension of value delivery:

  • Investment performance: Net returns across standardised investment strategies, reported over five and ten year periods. This is the most commercially sensitive element — schemes that have underperformed comparable strategies will face direct market comparison for the first time.
  • Costs and charges: Total cost disclosures, including transaction costs, fund management charges, and administrative costs. The framework requires these to be presented in a way that permits meaningful comparison, not buried in product disclosure documents.
  • Quality of services: Administration quality, communication standards, the range of at-retirement options available, and how effectively the scheme engages with members throughout the savings journey. This pillar is the hardest to quantify, but TPR has made clear it will not be reduced to a tick-box exercise.

Schemes are required to assess themselves against each pillar, and crucially, to compare their performance against a set of market benchmarks. Where a scheme falls into the lowest tier — red — on any pillar, it will be expected to either demonstrate a credible improvement plan or initiate a transfer to a better-performing arrangement.

Benchmarking, disclosure, and public comparisons

The most significant departure from previous practice is the public nature of the new framework. Under the VFM regime, scheme-level data will be aggregated into a publicly accessible comparison tool. Employers choosing a default pension provider for their workforce will, for the first time, have a regulated benchmark against which to assess their existing arrangements. This is a structural change to the competitive environment — not merely a compliance exercise.

"Value for money assessment is no longer a self-marking exercise. Public benchmarking means that underperformance is visible — to employers, to members, and to regulators simultaneously."

Who is most exposed

The framework primarily targets occupational DC schemes and master trusts, reflecting TPR's primary jurisdiction. However, contract-based workplace pensions fall within the FCA's parallel framework and the overall VFM architecture is designed to be consistent across both. For DB schemes, the immediate VFM obligations are distinct — the framework is DC-centric — but the broader value agenda is beginning to touch DB through the lens of member outcomes at retirement and transfer value adequacy.

Within the DC market, the schemes with the most to do are typically smaller, single-employer schemes that have not consolidated into a master trust. Many of these arrangements carry higher per-member costs and have neither the investment scale nor the governance infrastructure to match the largest providers. The VFM framework makes explicit what the consolidation argument has been suggesting for years: if you cannot demonstrate competitive value, the expectation is that you should be moving your members somewhere that can.

Red-rating consequences

A scheme that receives a red rating under the VFM framework and cannot produce a credible improvement plan may face regulatory action — including TPR's use of its powers to direct consolidation. This is a material escalation from previous supervisory practice.

How to prepare

Schemes approaching VFM compliance should begin with an honest audit of their current data infrastructure. The framework requires granular, accurate investment performance data, clean cost disclosures, and a structured assessment of service quality. Many schemes will find that their data is not yet in a form that permits meaningful comparative benchmarking.

The governance steps are equally important. Trustees should commission an independent pre-assessment well before the regulatory deadline — allowing time to address data gaps, review investment strategies, and, if necessary, open a strategic review of the scheme's long-term viability as a standalone arrangement. Waiting for the formal regime to go live before beginning this work is a decision that will create avoidable pressure.

For those managing the data side of this challenge, the investment performance calculations alone require consistent, time-series data across multiple strategy types and comparison classes. Getting this right requires both the right data infrastructure and the analytical capacity to interpret it correctly — areas where many scheme administrators acknowledge they currently have gaps.

Key takeaways

  1. VFM assessment is becoming regulatory obligation — not best practice. Schemes that cannot demonstrate value face real consequences.
  2. Three pillars: investment performance, costs, and service quality — all must be assessed against standardised market benchmarks.
  3. Public comparison data changes competitive dynamics — employers and members will be able to see how their scheme ranks against alternatives.
  4. Smaller single-employer DC schemes face the greatest exposure — the framework is designed, in part, to accelerate consolidation among underperformers.
  5. Data infrastructure is the critical preparation step — schemes without clean, comparative performance data need to act now.
