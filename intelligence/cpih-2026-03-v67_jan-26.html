What CPIH is and why it matters for pensions

The Consumer Prices Index including owner occupiers' housing costs (CPIH) is the ONS's most comprehensive measure of UK consumer price inflation. Published monthly, it extends the standard CPI basket to include an estimate of the cost of housing services for owner-occupiers — making it arguably a more representative measure of the inflationary pressures facing UK households than CPI alone. The Bank of England targets CPI at 2%, but CPIH is increasingly used as a reference measure by researchers and policymakers tracking the full cost-of-living picture.

For pension schemes, inflation data is operationally significant. Many DB schemes are legally required to increase pensions in payment and deferred benefits in line with inflation measures — whether CPI, RPI, or scheme-specific definitions. Inflation-linked increases are not discretionary; they represent a legally accrued benefit obligation. When inflation runs persistently above target, those obligations are higher than actuarial models built on long-run mean reversion might have anticipated, with direct cash flow consequences for scheme sponsors and trustees.

The January 2026 reading in context

The CPIH index for January 2026 stands at 139.4, down from 139.9 in December 2025 — a monthly fall of 0.36%. The annual rate of CPIH inflation is 3.2%, unchanged from the previous month. The month-on-month decline reflects seasonal factors, principally the reversal of Christmas-period price increases in clothing, footwear, and recreational goods. However, the annual rate remaining at 3.2% confirms that underlying inflationary pressure continues to run above the Bank of England's 2% target.

Housing costs — the component that distinguishes CPIH from CPI — remain elevated, reflecting the ongoing impact of mortgage rate increases that worked through the rental market during 2024 and 2025. Services inflation, which is closely watched by the MPC as a signal of domestically-generated price pressure, is also persistently above target. The January reading does not suggest an imminent return to target.

Pension increases linked to inflation

The legal framework for inflation-linked pension increases in DB schemes is set out in the Pensions Act 1995 and subsequent legislation. The minimum statutory requirement is for pensions in payment accrued after April 1997 to increase annually by the lower of CPI and 5% — a mechanism known as Limited Price Indexation (LPI). Deferred pensions must revalue in line with either CPI or a fixed rate, depending on the scheme rules and the date of accrual.

With CPI running close to CPIH at around 3%, January 2026 data points to a pension increase round that will be meaningfully above average for schemes where last year's low revaluation built in optimistic assumptions. Schemes with significant proportions of pensions that revalue without a cap — whether through generous scheme rules or RPI-linked legacy benefits — face correspondingly higher liability growth.

LPI caps and floors — where they bite

The interaction between prevailing inflation and the LPI cap structure matters significantly in practice. For schemes where the LPI cap is 5%, a CPI rate of 3.2% means the full inflationary increase applies — the cap is not binding. For schemes where benefits are linked to RPI rather than CPI (typically older accruals), the increase will be somewhat higher still: RPI typically runs 0.5 to 1 percentage point above CPI due to methodological differences, and the divergence has been a persistent feature of the UK inflation landscape since the ONS reclassified RPI as a non-official statistic.

Floors are less discussed but also operationally significant. Most scheme rules contain a 0% floor — pensions do not decrease in deflationary conditions. In the current environment the floor is not binding, but it represents an embedded option that actuaries should factor into long-run liability modelling, particularly for schemes with prolonged low-inflation assumptions in their technical provisions.

What schemes should review

Trustees and scheme executives should use the January 2026 reading to verify that their administration platform is applying the correct increase basis to each tranche of member benefits. Complex DB schemes may contain multiple increase bases — pre-1997 accruals with no statutory requirement to increase, post-1997 accruals with LPI 5% caps, post-2005 accruals with LPI 2.5% caps, and potentially RPI-linked legacy benefits — all applied simultaneously at the April annual review. Getting this wrong creates underpayment or overpayment errors that are costly to identify and rectify.

Cash flow projections should also be updated to reflect the current inflationary environment. Schemes that modelled pension increase costs on a 2% long-run CPI assumption are now operating in a 3.2% environment. The difference between these assumptions compounds over time — a meaningful revision to projected outgoings for schemes with large pensioner payrolls.

Key takeaways

  1. CPIH annual rate is 3.2% in January 2026 — index at 139.4, with a small monthly fall of 0.36% reflecting seasonal factors.
  2. Above-target inflation means meaningful DB pension increases — the April revaluation round will be materially above long-run average for most schemes.
  3. LPI cap structure determines who pays the full rate — for schemes with a 5% cap, 3.2% CPI means no cap relief; the full increase applies.
  4. RPI-linked benefits will increase faster than CPI-linked — RPI typically runs 0.5–1pp above CPI, widening the cost differential for schemes with legacy RPI obligations.
  5. Administration accuracy is critical — complex benefit structures require careful verification that each tranche of benefit is being increased on the correct legal basis.
