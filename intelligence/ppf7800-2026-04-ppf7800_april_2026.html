What the PPF 7800 Index Measures

The PPF 7800 Index is the Pension Protection Fund's monthly measure of the aggregate funding position across the approximately 5,000 defined benefit (DB) schemes eligible for PPF protection. It compares total scheme assets against Section 179 liabilities — the estimated cost of securing PPF-level member benefits with an insurer. The index is the primary public benchmark for UK DB pension health and is closely watched by trustees, sponsors, advisers, and policymakers alike.

The index is updated monthly using a roll-forward model. Asset returns are applied to the prior-month asset base, and liabilities are adjusted for changes in corporate bond yields (which drive the discount rate applied to future benefit cashflows), as well as shifts in RPI inflation expectations — a key determinant of the cost of inflation-linked benefits. The scheme universe itself changes over time as new schemes enter, schemes complete buyouts and exit, or sponsor insolvencies trigger PPF assessments. This means the total asset and liability figures are not always directly comparable month to month at face value.

The April 2026 Picture

April 2026 shows a modest softening from the March position: the aggregate funding ratio edges down to 130.8% from 131.0%, and the aggregate surplus contracts from £273.7bn to £263.8bn. Yet total assets rose significantly — from £1,123.1bn to £1,161.3bn — reflecting either positive asset returns or changes in the scheme universe. The parallel rise in liabilities, from £857.5bn to £887.6bn, is the dominant factor pulling the ratio lower. A modest compression in long-dated gilt yields during April would explain the liability increase, as lower discount rates mechanically inflate the present value of future benefit payments.

The picture should still be read as fundamentally strong. A 130.8% aggregate ratio remains historically exceptional. The UK DB universe has spent the better part of the last decade at ratios well below 100% on many measures; the sustained surplus above 130% over multiple consecutive months represents a structural shift, not a temporary blip. The £9.9bn reduction in the headline surplus — roughly a 3.6% narrowing — is a normal monthly fluctuation and not a signal of deteriorating conditions.

"Assets rose by nearly £40bn in April — yet the ratio slipped marginally. This is the index doing exactly what it should: showing trustees where liability movements create risk, even in a surplus environment."

Understanding the Asset-Liability Divergence

The divergence between rising assets and rising liabilities in April illustrates the importance of liability-driven investment (LDI) in the current environment. Schemes that hedged their interest rate exposure — using gilt repos, swaps, or pooled LDI funds — will have seen their liabilities and their hedge instruments move in tandem, keeping their net funding position relatively stable. Schemes with a lower hedge ratio will have seen their liabilities rise faster than their assets over the month, eroding some of their hard-won surplus. For trustees with residual unhedged liability exposure, April serves as a reminder that the current favourable position is not automatically self-sustaining.

Inflation expectations also feed into the April numbers. The Section 179 liability calculation includes the cost of future inflation-linked pension increases — typically capped CPI or RPI depending on the benefit structure. Any uptick in long-run inflation expectations increases the present value of those inflation-linked cashflows. Given that UK CPI remained stickier than expected through early 2026, schemes with higher proportions of uncapped or RPI-linked benefits carry more inflation sensitivity than peers with CPI-capped structures.

📊
Surplus context

Despite the £9.9bn month-on-month reduction, April 2026's £263.8bn surplus is still more than double the aggregate surplus recorded in early 2021, when the DB universe sat around £100bn in surplus on the same measure. The direction of travel over a five-year horizon remains strongly positive.

The Buyout Market in Q2 2026

The bulk annuity market is absorbing record volumes as scheme after scheme reaches buyout affordability. In this environment, capacity constraints are a real consideration: the major insurers — Legal & General, Aviva, Just Group, and Pension Insurance Corporation — have finite capacity to underwrite new business in any given quarter, and demand from well-funded schemes is high. Schemes that are best prepared — with clean member data, settled benefit specifications, and an adviser team experienced in insurer negotiations — are securing pricing that would have been unthinkable five years ago. Those that delay risk finding capacity tighter or pricing less favourable when they eventually come to market.

For smaller schemes below around £50m in assets, the streamlined or "standardised" buyout products now offered by several insurers have made the process more accessible. The process that once required 18-24 months of preparation can now be completed in significantly less time for a well-run smaller scheme. Trustees of these schemes should not assume buyout is only for the large; competition for smaller mandates is increasing and pricing has improved considerably.

What Scheme Trustees Should Do Now

Key Actions

  1. Assess your hedge ratio in light of the April liability movement. If your scheme lacks meaningful interest rate hedging, quantify the cost of bringing it up — the surplus window may not stay open indefinitely.
  2. Review inflation sensitivity by benefit type. Schemes with large RPI-linked tranches face different risks than those with capped CPI increases; understanding which members hold which benefit structures is essential.
  3. Engage a bulk annuity adviser if you have not already. Even a preliminary market sounding costs nothing and gives trustees a clear picture of current insurer appetite and indicative pricing.
  4. Update your scheme data. Insurer due diligence is comprehensive; unresolved GMP equalisation, address gaps, or benefit queries will add time and cost to any transaction.
  5. Document your endgame framework in the statement of investment principles. The Pensions Regulator increasingly expects trustees to have a clearly articulated, timestamped endgame strategy.
