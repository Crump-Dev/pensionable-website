Innovate to deliver in the new pensions era – Nausicaa Delfas

20 March 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 6 min read

The regulatory shift: from scheme-level compliance to system-wide outcomes

Nausicaa Delfas became Chief Executive of The Pensions Regulator in 2023, arriving from the Financial Conduct Authority where she had led international regulation. Her tenure has been defined by a conscious repositioning of TPR — away from the reactive, scheme-by-scheme compliance model that characterised the post-Maxwell era and towards a more outcome-focused, prudential style of regulation more familiar in financial services.

The speech she delivered to the JP Morgan Pensions and Savings Symposium on 20 March 2026 is the clearest statement yet of what that shift means in practice. Speaking to an audience of institutional investors, fund managers, and industry innovators, Delfas did not present a list of enforcement priorities. Instead, she set out a vision for a transformed pension system — and then made explicit that the industry is expected to help build it.

The context is the Pension Schemes Bill, currently progressing through Parliament, alongside the work of the renewed Pensions Commission. These are generational reforms. Automatic enrolment — the policy success that brought over 22 million people into workplace pensions — solved the accumulation problem. The current legislative programme is designed to solve what comes next: ensuring those pots are invested well, governed properly, and eventually converted into income that people can actually live on.

"We are moving out of one era and into another. We can't go back to an era where the image of retirement was a gold watch and a handshake — but we can create a future where people benefit from better performing schemes, with more transparent information, with clear default options at retirement."

Nausicaa Delfas, Chief Executive, TPR — JP Morgan Pensions and Savings Symposium, 20 March 2026

What "innovate to deliver" means operationally

The speech's central demand is that the industry stop treating innovation as a peripheral concern and recognise it as core to delivering the regulator's vision. Delfas framed this across three distinct areas, each with specific expectations for what market participants should be providing.

For defined benefit schemes, the message is that funding surpluses are creating choices that did not previously exist. With DB scheme assets collectively holding around a trillion pounds and roughly half of schemes fully funded on a buyout basis, the era of existential DB funding stress is largely over. The question now is what trustees and employers do with that position. Delfas identified three broad archetypes: well-funded schemes seeking insurer buyout; large well-funded schemes choosing to run on and extract surplus for member benefit or economic investment; and underfunded schemes for which superfund consolidation may provide the right scale and security. Her call to market participants was explicit — produce the products and services each of those segments actually needs, rather than defaulting to a one-size approach.

For defined contribution schemes, the priority is moving beyond cost-driven decision-making. Delfas pointed to the value for money framework — to be introduced through the Bill — as the mechanism for enabling trustees to evaluate schemes on investment outcomes rather than just fees. She cited the compound impact: for an average saver starting at 22, a 1% improvement in investment returns over a working life means a 30% larger pot at retirement. At the aggregate level, better value for money has the potential to deliver almost £20 billion in benefits over the next decade.

Technology, data governance, and the AI signal

The speech devoted significant attention to administration, data, and technology — and it is here that Delfas's language is most pointed. She described the industry as "ripe for innovation" but immediately qualified this: innovation can only succeed if it is built on solid foundations of strong governance, administration, and data quality.

The specific challenges she named are familiar to anyone operating within a pension scheme: legacy systems that cannot easily communicate with new technology; uneven investment in administration infrastructure across the market; inconsistent data quality that will be exposed by the upcoming pensions dashboards; and variability in administrative performance that creates real risk for members.

📊
The scale of the data challenge

TPR's new DC reserving guidance, published alongside the speech, allows some master trusts to reduce cash reserves and deploy capital more efficiently. But the broader data agenda — feeding pensions dashboards and enabling AI — requires schemes to tackle data quality at the record level. That is a governance task, not a technology task.

On AI specifically, Delfas signalled that TPR's AI Plan would follow in May (it did — see our coverage of the AI Plan). The speech foreshadowed the principles that would appear in that document: AI must be used responsibly, with governance and member outcomes at the centre. She also flagged the risks: the advice-guidance boundary, digital inclusion, and fraud. The regulator's approach, she said, would be "outcome-focused and technology-agnostic" — a deliberate framing that avoids prescribing specific tools while making clear that the outcome of safe, member-first AI adoption is non-negotiable.

The risk of standing still

One of the more significant passages in the speech was Delfas's candid acknowledgement that compliance-focused regulation is no longer sufficient. For most of TPR's history, ensuring schemes complied with funding rules was the primary mechanism for protecting members. In a DB world, compliance broadly equalled security. That model does not translate to the DC world, where the member outcome depends on investment quality, administration accuracy, and the ability to convert a pot into sustainable income — none of which is guaranteed by compliance alone.

The implication for schemes is that regulatory comfort cannot be derived simply from ticking compliance boxes. TPR is looking outwards at system-wide risks, macroeconomic conditions, and the relationships between entities in the market. Schemes that are merely compliant but not delivering value — through poor governance, weak administration, or failure to engage with the reform agenda — are likely to attract more scrutiny under the new approach, not less.

Delfas also made concrete the cost of inaction on data and administration. Pensions dashboards will direct millions of savers' attention to their pots in a way that has never happened before. If the data those dashboards surface is wrong — because of poor record-keeping, legacy system errors, or unreconciled transfers — the reputational and operational consequences for schemes will be significant. The time to fix data is now, not when a saver calls to ask why their dashboard shows a value that does not match their benefit statement.

Takeaways for trustees and scheme managers

The speech is not enforcement guidance, and it contains no new regulatory requirements in itself. But it is a clear statement of direction from the person responsible for setting TPR's agenda. Trustees and scheme administrators should read it as a signal, not a suggestion.

Key takeaways for trustees and administrators

  1. Understand your scheme's position in the new landscape. Whether you are a DB trustee weighing endgame options or a DC trustee reviewing investment strategy, the regulatory environment is changing in ways that require active engagement, not passive compliance.
  2. Treat administration as a strategic function. Delfas explicitly called for schemes to view administrators as strategic partners, not cost centres. That means investment in data quality, system modernisation, and governance — not just contractual compliance.
  3. Engage with the value for money framework proactively. The framework is coming. Schemes that have already started the internal work of understanding their investment outcomes relative to peers will be better positioned when comparative data is published publicly.
  4. Use TPR's innovation service. The regulator relaunched its innovation support service in 2025 specifically to provide early engagement for schemes and providers considering new models or technologies. This is a genuine dialogue opportunity, particularly for those exploring AI-assisted processes ahead of the forthcoming AI guidance.
