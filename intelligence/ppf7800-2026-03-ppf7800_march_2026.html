What the PPF 7800 Index Measures

The PPF 7800 Index is published monthly by the Pension Protection Fund and tracks the aggregate funding position of the approximately 5,000 defined benefit (DB) pension schemes eligible for PPF protection in the United Kingdom. It takes its name from the Pension Schemes Act section that underpins PPF protection, and it is the most widely cited barometer of DB pension health in the country. The index measures assets against Section 179 liabilities — the cost of securing PPF-level benefits with an insurer — rather than full buyout or technical provisions, which means it tends to show a more conservative picture than a scheme's own actuarial valuation.

Each month, the PPF updates the index using a roll-forward model based on asset returns, changes in gilt yields (which directly affect the present value of liabilities), and any changes to the scheme universe. Because liabilities are discounted using corporate bond yields — proxied through gilt yields and a spread — movements in long-dated gilts have an outsized effect on the index from one month to the next. This makes the 7800 Index not just a funding measure but an implicit tracker of interest rate risk across the UK's DB landscape.

The March 2026 Picture

The March 2026 release shows an aggregate funding ratio of 131.0%, with total assets of £1,123.1bn set against Section 179 liabilities of £857.5bn. The resulting aggregate surplus of £273.7bn represents one of the strongest funding positions recorded since the index began. This marks a continuation of the structural improvement that accelerated sharply from late 2022, when the rapid rise in gilt yields — itself triggered by the Bank of England's rate tightening cycle — compressed liability values significantly faster than assets fell.

In practical terms, a 131% aggregate ratio means that if every PPF-eligible DB scheme were assessed for entry to the PPF today, the pool of assets across the universe would comfortably exceed the cost of covering all members' protected benefits. Fewer than a third of schemes are now estimated to be in deficit on a Section 179 basis — a transformation from the mid-2010s, when the majority were underfunded and the PPF itself faced substantial long-run risk.

What Drives the Funding Ratio

The two dominant forces on funding ratios are gilt yields and asset returns. When long-dated gilt yields rise, the present value of future pension liabilities falls — because those future cashflows are discounted at a higher rate. This is precisely what happened through 2022 and 2023: a 300-basis-point rise in 20-year gilt yields translated into a roughly 40% reduction in the discounted value of liabilities for a typical scheme with a long duration profile. Schemes that had implemented Liability-Driven Investment (LDI) strategies — using interest rate swaps and gilt-based instruments to hedge their liability sensitivity — benefited most cleanly from this dynamic, seeing their funding positions hold firm or improve even as assets delivered modest returns.

By March 2026, the Bank Rate has stabilised at 3.75% following a gradual cutting cycle that began in late 2024. Long-dated gilt yields remain elevated by historical standards, which continues to suppress liability values and maintain the healthy aggregate surplus position. Schemes with robust LDI programmes in place are well-insulated against a further moderate fall in yields; those without meaningful interest rate hedging face residual exposure if rates decline significantly from current levels.

Surplus context

At £273.7bn, the March 2026 aggregate surplus is among the highest on record for the PPF 7800 Index. Compare this with 2020, when aggregate deficits exceeded £100bn — a shift of roughly £375bn in the aggregate position over five years, driven primarily by the 2022-2023 gilt yield spike and subsequent LDI-driven liability compression.

Endgame Options in a Surplus World

Surplus funding at scheme level — not just in the aggregate — changes the strategic calculus for trustees and sponsoring employers. The primary endgame route has historically been bulk annuity buyout, where an insurer takes on the liabilities in exchange for a premium representing the full cost of securing benefits. At current funding levels, many schemes find they can approach the buyout market without requiring additional contributions from the sponsor, which removes a longstanding barrier. The UK bulk annuity market has seen record volumes in recent years, with insurers such as Legal & General, Aviva, Just Group, and PIC competing actively for well-prepared mandates.

Buyout is not the only option. Run-on — where a scheme continues independently, with the sponsor retaining the economic benefit of surplus returns — is increasingly viable for larger, well-governed schemes. The Pension Schemes Act 2021 introduced a superfund regime as an alternative consolidation route for schemes whose sponsors cannot support buyout. And regulatory developments around surplus extraction have opened conversations about returning value to sponsoring employers where scheme security is demonstrably strong. For trustees, the right path depends on scheme maturity, sponsor covenant, member profile, and risk appetite.

What Scheme Trustees Should Do Now

Key Actions

  1. Review your funding position against the PPF 7800 aggregate to understand where your scheme sits relative to peers — whether you are above or below the 131% aggregate ratio matters for pace of action.
  2. Commission a buyout affordability assessment if you have not done so recently. Insurer pricing has tightened significantly; a broker-led feasibility study will confirm whether a transaction is now within reach.
  3. Stress-test your LDI hedge ratio to understand residual liability sensitivity to a 50-100bp fall in gilt yields — particularly relevant given the Bank of England's easing cycle is not yet complete.
  4. Engage your sponsor on endgame strategy. Surplus positions alter the power dynamic; sponsor and trustee interests may now be better aligned than at any point in the last decade.
  5. Check your data and benefit accuracy. Insurers conduct detailed due diligence, and errors in member data or benefit specifications will delay and reprice a buyout transaction.
