Master Trusts dominate as smaller schemes continue to exit the defined contribution (DC) market

17 March 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 5 min read

The consolidation picture

New data published by The Pensions Regulator confirms what the industry has watched building for several years: the defined contribution landscape is now overwhelmingly dominated by master trusts. The number of DC trust-based schemes has fallen sharply over the past decade, from more than 2,500 in the mid-2010s to well under 1,000 today — and the pace of exit shows little sign of slowing. Meanwhile, the master trust sector has grown in both scheme count and assets, with the largest providers now collectively holding hundreds of billions of pounds on behalf of tens of millions of members.

TPR's figures show that master trusts now account for the vast majority of active DC membership in the trust-based market. The top ten master trusts by membership — including Nest, the People's Pension, Smart Pension, Legal & General Mastertrust, and Aegon Master Trust — between them serve the bulk of auto-enrolment savers. Nest alone exceeded 12 million members by the mid-2020s, a scale that would have been unimaginable before automatic enrolment was introduced in 2012.

Why smaller schemes are leaving

The drivers behind the exodus are structural, not cyclical. Running a small to medium-sized DC scheme has become an increasingly difficult proposition. Governance requirements introduced under the Pension Schemes Act 2021 and TPR's General Code — which came into force in 2024 — demand that trustees maintain effective systems of governance covering everything from risk management and internal controls to cyber security and member data quality. For a scheme with a few thousand members and a modest assets base, meeting this bar requires a disproportionate investment of trustee time and professional fees.

The cost-per-member arithmetic is stark. A master trust spreading compliance costs across millions of savers can run the same governance infrastructure at a fraction of the per-head cost compared to a standalone scheme with 500 members. Investment governance compounds the problem: smaller schemes struggle to access the diversified, illiquid asset classes — infrastructure, private equity, private credit — that larger pools can access and which are increasingly central to the government's productive finance agenda.

Member outcome data has reinforced the case. TPR's value for members assessments have repeatedly highlighted that sub-scale DC schemes deliver inferior net-of-cost returns. Trustees who cannot demonstrate that their scheme provides good value against a master trust comparator now face active regulatory pressure to consolidate.

"Running a DC scheme well requires genuine scale. Below a certain threshold, the economics simply do not support the governance investment that members deserve."

What master trusts offer

The appeal of master trusts is not simply cost. The best-run providers have invested heavily in member experience — digital dashboards, retirement planning tools, income drawdown functionality, and proactive engagement programmes. They carry dedicated in-house investment teams, fiduciary managers, and administration platforms built specifically for large-scale DC. For an employer, transferring to a master trust removes the complexity of trustee recruitment and training, scheme administration oversight, and the risk of regulatory censure for governance failures.

The diversification advantages are significant too. Master trusts with assets in excess of £5 billion can access the kind of long-term illiquid investments — UK infrastructure, life science property, private credit facilities — that the government has been actively encouraging pension schemes to consider. The Mansion House Compact, signed by major master trust providers in 2023, committed signatories to allocate at least 5% of default fund assets to unlisted equities by 2030. Smaller single-employer schemes cannot practically participate in commitments of this nature.

Regulatory checkpoint: TPR's value for members framework requires DC trustees to compare their scheme's costs, charges, investment performance, and services against an appropriate master trust benchmark at least every three years. Schemes that cannot demonstrate good value must explain what steps they are taking to address the gap — or consider winding up.

The regulatory view

TPR has been explicit in its consolidation ambitions. The regulator's long-term strategy targets a DC market made up of a small number of well-governed, large-scale providers — with single-employer trust-based schemes reserved for situations where there is a demonstrable and sustained member benefit from remaining standalone. That is a high bar in practice. Regulatory guidance makes clear that inertia — continuing to run a scheme simply because winding up is complicated — is not an acceptable justification for remaining in the market.

The Pensions (Extension of Automatic Enrolment) Act 2023 expanded auto-enrolment eligibility, pushing more contributions into the DC market over the coming years. This further inflates the scale advantage of master trusts and increases the governance burden on smaller schemes that receive new joiners under broadened enrolment rules. TPR has signalled it will use its existing enforcement toolkit — including improvement notices and compliance notices — against trustees who knowingly persist with schemes that fail the value-for-members test.

What small DC schemes should do now

For trustees still running standalone DC arrangements, the March 2026 data release is a prompt — not a death notice. The right response is a structured review, not a panic. Trustees should begin by commissioning a value for members assessment that benchmarks their scheme against at least two master trust alternatives on charges, net returns, member services, and governance quality. The output should be discussed at trustee board level with a clear decision recorded in meeting minutes.

If the outcome of that review points toward consolidation, the process is well-trodden. Master trust providers have dedicated transition teams, and the market for bulk transfer advice is competitive. Employers should be involved early: they have legitimate interests in the choice of receiving scheme, the transfer timeline, and member communications. Trustees should also consider whether a phased approach — closing to new entrants while existing members transfer — is more appropriate than an immediate full wind-up.

Schemes that conclude they can still demonstrate good value should document that case thoroughly. The evidential bar is rising each year as master trust capabilities advance. A scheme that passes the assessment in 2026 may not pass it in 2029. Building a forward-looking review schedule into trustee governance plans is prudent risk management.

Key takeaways

  • Master trusts now dominate DC membership; single-employer schemes continue to fall in number
  • Governance costs, scale economics, and investment access are driving the consolidation
  • TPR's value for members framework is the primary lever — trustees must benchmark against master trusts regularly
  • Inertia is not an acceptable justification for remaining standalone; TPR can and will act
  • Trustees should commission a formal benchmarking review and document the outcome at board level
