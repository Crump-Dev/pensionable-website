Fraud Minister calls on trustees to use every touchpoint to protect savers from pension scams

16 April 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 5 min read

Ministerial intervention in pension scheme administration is uncommon. When the Fraud Minister addresses trustees directly — calling on them to embed scam warnings into every communication they send to members — it signals that pension fraud has risen to the level of a government priority, not merely a regulatory concern. Trustees and administrators should treat this intervention accordingly: as a statement of political intent that is likely to inform TPR's forthcoming supervisory activity.

The core message is straightforward, but its implications require careful unpacking. "Every touchpoint" is not a vague aspiration. It is a specific instruction to use the full breadth of scheme-to-member communications — not just dedicated fraud alerts, but retirement quotations, annual benefit statements, transfer acknowledgements, and life-event correspondence — as active channels for scam education and warning.

Government Raises the Stakes on Pension Scam Protection

The Fraud Minister's involvement reflects a broader shift in how government views pension fraud. For much of the past decade, scam prevention in the pensions sector has been framed primarily as a regulatory and consumer protection matter — the domain of TPR and the FCA, operating through campaigns like ScamSmart and through the transfer due-diligence rules introduced under the Occupational and Personal Pension Schemes (Conditions for Transfers) Regulations 2021.

That framing is changing. Pension fraud now sits within a wider government fraud strategy that treats it as a serious economic and social harm, not a niche financial regulation issue. The scale justifies the attention: the FCA and TPR estimate that tens of thousands of savers are at risk of pension fraud at any given time, and that actual reported losses — which almost certainly understate the true figure — run into hundreds of millions of pounds annually.

Ministerial involvement also raises the question of what follows. Schemes that are seen to be doing the minimum — including a boilerplate sentence about scams at the foot of a transfer form — should not assume that satisfies the spirit of the minister's call. TPR has consistently signalled that it expects trustees to approach member protection actively, not defensively.

The scale of the risk

FCA and TPR data suggests hundreds of millions of pounds are lost to pension fraud annually in the UK. Defined benefit transfer values — often exceeding £100,000 per member — make occupational scheme members a high-value target. A single successful scam can wipe out decades of retirement savings.

What "Every Touchpoint" Means in Practice

The phrase "every touchpoint" is doing significant work in the minister's message. For schemes that have not yet mapped their member communications against scam protection obligations, it is worth doing so explicitly. The key touchpoints and what the minister's call implies for each are as follows.

Retirement quotation packs. Members approaching retirement and requesting a quote are at a particularly high-risk moment. Fraudsters monitor retirement activity and target members who are actively thinking about their pension. Retirement packs should include prominent, plain-English warnings about pension scams — not as a legal disclaimer, but as a genuine alert tailored to the decisions the member is about to make.

Annual benefit statements. These reach every active and deferred member, typically once a year. They represent one of the few guaranteed points of contact between scheme and member. A dedicated section — not a footnote — addressing scam risks and directing members to ScamSmart or MoneyHelper is a proportionate and practical use of this channel.

Transfer request acknowledgements. When a member initiates a transfer, the scheme is legally required to conduct due diligence under the 2021 transfer regulations. But the ministerial message asks for more than procedural compliance. The acknowledgement itself — the first communication the member receives after requesting a transfer — is an opportunity to prompt reflection: has this transfer been initiated freely, without pressure, after independent financial advice?

Death and bereavement notifications. Bereaved dependants and nominees are a known target for fraudsters, who monitor probate records and pension fund announcements. Communications to dependants should include scam awareness information, particularly if the dependant has not previously been a scheme member themselves.

Address and contact detail update confirmations. Address changes are a common precursor to fraudulent transfer requests — scammers who have obtained member credentials through impersonation will often change the registered address before initiating a transfer. Confirmation letters should include a prompt for the member to contact the scheme if the change was not requested by them.

The Regulatory Obligation for Trustees

The ministerial message is framed as a call to action rather than a legislative mandate — but trustees should understand that its regulatory underpinning is already in place. The Occupational and Personal Pension Schemes (Conditions for Transfers) Regulations 2021 require trustees to identify "amber flags" — including unusual transfer routes and member contact by third parties — and to refer affected members to guidance from MoneyHelper before proceeding with a transfer. These obligations are not optional, and TPR has enforcement powers where schemes fail to meet them.

Beyond the transfer regulations, TPR's broader expectations of trustees — articulated in its trustee toolkit and in its guidance on member communications — already contemplate scam awareness as a component of good member outcomes. The ministerial statement reinforces and accelerates those expectations. Schemes that have not yet reviewed their communications against this standard should treat the April 2026 statement as a prompt to do so.

There is also a fiduciary dimension. Trustees' duty to act in members' best interests extends to taking reasonable steps to protect members from foreseeable harm. A member who is defrauded of their pension savings following a transfer that the scheme processed without adequate warnings or due diligence has suffered a harm that the scheme may, in some circumstances, be seen as having contributed to. The legal and reputational risk is real.

Building a Scam-Aware Culture

Effective scam protection is not a communications project alone. The minister's call to use every touchpoint will only be effective if the scheme's people — administrators, member services staff, and trustees themselves — understand the threat and take it seriously. That requires investment in culture as much as process.

Staff training. Administration staff should receive regular, specific training on current scam typologies — not generic fraud awareness, but pension-specific scenarios including impersonation fraud, transfer scams, and unregulated investment enticements. Training should be documented, repeated at least annually, and updated when the threat landscape changes.

Scripted responses. Front-line staff benefit from having pre-agreed responses to common scam-related situations: a member who says they have been contacted by someone claiming to be from the scheme, a member who is being pressured to transfer, a caller who is unusually insistent about the urgency of a request. Scripted responses reduce the cognitive burden on individual staff and ensure consistency.

Escalation procedures. Any member contact that displays warning signs — pressure, unusual transfer routes, requests for personal data — should be escalatable immediately, with a clear path to a senior administrator or trustee. Staff should feel supported in pausing or refusing a request, backed by documented policy and trustee endorsement.

Post-incident review. When a suspicious contact is identified — or when a member reports that they believe they may have been targeted — there should be a structured review process. What happened? What information was shared? What further steps are needed to protect the member? This process also generates valuable intelligence about evolving tactics that can feed back into training and communications.

Implementing touchpoint warnings: key actions for trustees

  1. Map every member communication against scam protection. List all documents and correspondence your scheme sends to members and assess whether each one currently carries a scam warning. Identify gaps and prioritise the highest-risk touchpoints — retirement packs and transfer acknowledgements — for immediate update.
  2. Review your transfer due-diligence procedures against the 2021 regulations. Confirm that your amber-flag identification process is documented, operational, and understood by the staff who handle transfer requests. Check that MoneyHelper referrals are being made where required and that records are kept.
  3. Add scam-awareness content to annual benefit statements. Include a dedicated, visible section — not a footnote — directing members to ScamSmart and MoneyHelper, and summarising the key red flags in plain English.
  4. Develop and deploy staff training specific to pension fraud. Generic fraud awareness is not sufficient. Training should cover the specific scenarios trustees' members are likely to encounter, and it should be refreshed at least annually.
  5. Establish a documented escalation path and log suspicious contacts. Formalise the route by which staff can escalate a suspicious contact, and create a log of such events. This supports pattern detection, demonstrates compliance, and protects staff who take the right decision to pause a request.

The Fraud Minister's intervention is a clear signal that the pensions sector is expected to step up. Schemes that treat member scam protection as a compliance exercise — ticking the box and moving on — are out of step with where both government and regulator now stand. The ask is not unreasonable: to use the communications you already send, and the staff you already have, to make scam awareness a consistent thread running through everything your scheme says to its members.

Done well, this does not require significant additional resource. It requires intention, consistency, and the trustee-level commitment to make it happen.
