Master trusts: prepare for future scale requirements now

09 March 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 5 min read

The scale question

The Pensions Regulator has issued a clear signal to the master trust sector: do not wait for legislation before building the systems and governance structures that scale requirements will demand. In its March 2026 communication, TPR reiterated that formal minimum scale thresholds — covering both assets under management and member numbers — are coming, and that providers who begin preparing now will be better placed than those who delay until a statutory deadline forces their hand.

The concept of minimum scale thresholds is not new. The Pension Schemes Act 2021 gave ministers enabling powers to set such requirements by regulation, and DWP consulted on the framework in subsequent years. What is crystallising in 2026 is TPR's impatience with the pace of voluntary consolidation. Despite the clear economics favouring larger schemes, a number of smaller master trusts — authorised but with modest asset bases — have remained in operation without the infrastructure to compete on member outcomes. TPR wants this resolved, with or without a hard legislative trigger.

What TPR expects

TPR's message is directed at two groups: smaller master trusts that may fall below any threshold that is eventually set, and mid-tier providers that technically exceed any near-term threshold but lack the systems to operate at the scale they will need to reach. The regulator expects boards of all master trusts to be actively stress-testing their position against a range of plausible threshold scenarios — and to have a credible growth or consolidation plan documented and owned at board level.

For those unlikely to meet thresholds on their own trajectory, TPR has been explicit that proactive engagement with potential merger partners is preferable to a last-minute forced transfer. The authorisation regime for master trusts gives TPR powers to revoke authorisation if a scheme no longer meets the conditions — including the financial sustainability and systems and processes conditions. A scheme that loses authorisation faces a disorderly wind-up, which is among the worst outcomes for members.

"The direction of travel is clear. Scale thresholds are coming. The question for master trust boards is not whether to prepare, but how quickly."

Infrastructure requirements

Operating at genuine scale requires investment across several dimensions simultaneously. Administration platforms must be capable of processing millions of member records accurately, handling bulk transfers in, processing contributions from thousands of employers with different payroll cycles, and managing complex retirement income journeys — all without material error rates. The operational resilience requirements in TPR's General Code set a high bar: systems must be tested, backed up, and recoverable within defined timeframes.

Data quality is the foundation on which everything else rests. Master trusts absorbing transfers from smaller schemes frequently inherit poor data: missing National Insurance numbers, incorrect contribution histories, inaccurate employer references. Before any transfer, both the transferring and receiving scheme must have confidence in the accuracy of the data being moved. Schemes that have not invested in data cleansing programmes will find large-scale bulk transfers significantly more expensive and operationally risky than those that have.

Investment governance is the other major dimension. A master trust of genuine scale needs a robust investment committee, a clearly articulated investment strategy for default arrangements, a process for reviewing and adjusting the default, and the reporting infrastructure to demonstrate performance attribution and cost transparency to members. Smaller providers often rely heavily on a single investment manager or a delegated fiduciary arrangement with limited board oversight. That model becomes increasingly untenable as scheme size and complexity grow.

Why act before legislation

The temptation for master trust boards operating below their desired scale is to wait — for legislation to set the threshold, for the market to evolve further, for a strategic partner to emerge. TPR's message is that this logic is flawed on several levels. First, the regulatory and commercial environment for sub-scale trusts is already deteriorating: employers increasingly default to the largest providers when setting up auto-enrolment arrangements, and the pool of employers willing to join a smaller master trust is shrinking year by year.

Second, building the infrastructure needed to operate at scale takes time that a legislative deadline will not afford. Administration system upgrades, data migration programmes, and investment governance overhauls typically take 18 to 36 months to complete properly. A trust that begins this work in response to a regulatory ultimatum will be doing it under pressure, with reduced options and increased cost. Third, and most critically, the members already in these schemes deserve the same standard of service and governance as members of the largest providers. That obligation exists now, not only once a threshold is formally breached.

Authorisation risk: Master trusts that do not meet TPR's financial sustainability, fit and proper persons, or systems and processes conditions risk losing their authorisation. Loss of authorisation triggers a supervised wind-up under TPR oversight — a disruptive and costly process for members and employers alike. Proactive action is always preferable to enforcement-led change.

Key actions for master trust boards

TPR expects master trust boards to demonstrate active engagement with scale planning rather than passive monitoring. Boards should commission an honest internal audit of their current position against the likely threshold scenarios — both an assets-based threshold (which may be set at somewhere in the region of £5 billion or above for full operational viability) and a member-count threshold. That audit should consider the scheme's trajectory on organic growth alone versus growth with targeted employer partnerships.

Where organic growth is insufficient, boards should be assessing the merger and acquisition landscape. Several well-regarded master trusts have consolidated successfully in recent years, with the transactions generally resulting in improved outcomes for members on both sides through better scale economies and broader investment access. Legal, actuarial, and administration advisers with experience of master trust mergers are available, and the process — while complex — is navigable with the right preparation.

Finally, boards should ensure their member communications are ahead of any regulatory or structural changes. Members have a right to understand the governance of their pension savings, and a trustee board that has proactively engaged with scale questions — and communicated its strategy clearly — is in a much stronger position with both the regulator and its membership than one that appears to have been caught off guard.

Key takeaways

  • Formal scale thresholds for master trusts are coming; TPR wants preparation now, not at the legislative deadline
  • Administration infrastructure, data quality, and investment governance all require multi-year investment programmes
  • Sub-scale trusts face deteriorating employer appetite and rising governance costs regardless of when thresholds are set
  • Loss of authorisation is the worst-case scenario — proactive consolidation or growth planning avoids it
  • Boards should document a credible scale strategy and communicate it to members and TPR
