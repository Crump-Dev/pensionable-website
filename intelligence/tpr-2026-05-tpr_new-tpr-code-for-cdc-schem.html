New TPR code for CDC schemes

29 April 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 5 min read

Understanding CDC

Collective defined contribution — CDC — is a pension structure that sits between traditional defined benefit and individual defined contribution. Unlike DB, it makes no guaranteed promise of a specific pension amount. Unlike DC, it does not leave each member exposed individually to investment and longevity risk. Instead, CDC pools contributions from all members into a single collective fund, which is then managed with the aim of paying target income in retirement. If the fund performs well, target income is maintained or enhanced. If it underperforms, benefits are adjusted downward. The adjustment mechanism — governed by strict actuarial rules — is what distinguishes CDC from both DB and DC.

The concept has operated successfully in the Netherlands and Denmark for decades, where large occupational CDC funds have delivered stable, inflation-linked retirement incomes to millions of workers. The UK's path to CDC has been longer. After years of policy development, the Pension Schemes Act 2021 finally created the legislative framework for CDC schemes in the UK. The first regulations came into force in 2022, and the first authorised CDC scheme — Royal Mail's — began accepting contributions from postal workers in 2023. TPR's updated code, published in April 2026, builds on the experience of those early years and extends the regulatory framework to accommodate a broader range of CDC structures beyond single-employer schemes.

How CDC works

The mechanics of CDC require understanding a small number of key concepts. Members contribute during their working lives — as do employers, at agreed rates — into the collective fund. On retirement, members receive an income based on their target benefit, which is calculated by reference to their contribution history and an assumed rate of return. The target is not guaranteed: it is subject to annual actuarial review, and can be increased or decreased depending on the fund's financial position relative to its liabilities.

The actuarial valuation process is the engine of a CDC scheme. An independent scheme actuary must certify annually that the scheme's assets are sufficient to meet its benefit targets, taking into account a range of demographic and investment assumptions. If the funding ratio falls below a defined threshold, the trustees must implement a benefit adjustment — reducing benefits proportionally across all members — to restore solvency. If the scheme is in surplus relative to its targets, trustees may increase benefits. This adjustment mechanism replaces the employer covenant that underpins DB schemes: members are protected not by a sponsoring employer's promise but by the collective pool and its governance rules.

The investment strategy in CDC differs significantly from individual DC. Because the scheme has a long-term liability profile and can smooth short-term volatility through the adjustment mechanism, it can invest more aggressively in growth assets over the long term — without the sequencing risk that forces individual DC drawdown investors to de-risk at the wrong moment. This structural advantage is one of CDC's most compelling features, and the Mansion House productive finance agenda has explicitly highlighted CDC as a vehicle well-suited to long-term UK infrastructure investment.

"CDC offers something individual DC cannot: the pooling of longevity and investment risk across a large membership, delivering retirement income that is both more predictable and — in most scenarios — materially higher than an individual annuity or drawdown arrangement could provide."

What the new code requires

TPR's April 2026 code of practice for CDC schemes sets out expectations across four main areas. First, governance: CDC trustees must demonstrate that they have the skills and experience to oversee a scheme with a fundamentally different risk profile from either DB or DC. The trustee board must include individuals with expertise in CDC actuarial methodology, investment strategy, and member communications — areas where the skill sets differ from conventional pension governance. TPR has signalled it will assess these competencies as part of the authorisation process and ongoing supervision.

Second, actuarial certification: the code sets detailed requirements for the annual actuarial report, including the assumptions that actuaries must use for mortality, investment returns, and expenses. It specifies the format in which benefit adjustment decisions must be documented and communicated to members, and sets expectations for how scenarios must be stress-tested. The code introduces a requirement for the scheme actuary to confirm in writing that the adjustment mechanism has been correctly applied each year — a significant professional accountability measure.

Third, member communications: CDC introduces complexity that individual DC members are not accustomed to. Members need to understand that their target benefit may be adjusted, what factors drive those adjustments, and what recourse they have if they believe an adjustment has been incorrectly applied. The code requires trustees to produce an annual funding statement in plain English, explaining the scheme's financial position and the rationale for any benefit changes. This statement must be sent to all members — not just posted on a website — and must meet accessibility standards for members with disabilities or limited literacy.

Fourth, benefit specification and wind-up: the code addresses what happens if a CDC scheme needs to wind up — whether due to employer insolvency, a decision to consolidate with another scheme, or a sustained period of poor performance that has eroded member benefits to the point where continuation is not in members' interests. The wind-up process for CDC is more complex than for DC because the collective fund must be unwound in a way that treats all members equitably. TPR expects trustees to have a wind-up plan documented and reviewed as part of their annual governance cycle.

The Royal Mail precedent

Royal Mail's CDC scheme is the UK's proving ground. When it launched in 2023, it became the first large-scale CDC arrangement in the country — covering around 100,000 postal workers and replacing a legacy DC arrangement that members had found unsatisfactory. The scheme's design was the product of years of negotiation between Royal Mail management and the Communication Workers Union, with both parties recognising that a return to DB was not financially viable for the employer but that pure DC was leaving too much retirement risk on individual workers.

The early years of the scheme have provided TPR and the wider industry with real operational data: how actuarial valuations work at scale, how members respond to benefit adjustment communications, how the investment strategy performs against its targets in live market conditions. The April 2026 code reflects lessons from that operational experience, including refined guidance on how benefit adjustments should be communicated to members who may not understand why their expected retirement income has changed. The Royal Mail model has also catalysed interest from other large employers — particularly those in the public service contracting, utilities, and transport sectors — that see CDC as a viable alternative to rising DC contribution rates or the unaffordable cost of restoring DB benefits.

Is CDC right for your scheme?

CDC is not a universal solution. It requires scale — both in terms of member numbers and contribution flow — to function effectively. The collective pooling mechanism works best when the membership is large and demographically diverse, so that individual longevity shocks average out. A CDC scheme with fewer than several thousand active members may struggle to sustain the collective risk-sharing model, and may face higher per-member administration costs than a well-run master trust DC arrangement.

It also requires a committed employer who is willing to maintain consistent contribution rates over the long term. The adjustment mechanism protects the scheme from short-term market shocks, but persistent employer contribution underfunding would undermine the target benefit calculations and create a governance crisis. Employers considering CDC need to enter with a long-term perspective and a realistic assessment of their financial capacity to sustain the arrangement.

For large single employers — particularly unionised workforces where collective negotiation on pension design is feasible — and potentially for multi-employer industry-wide arrangements, CDC represents a genuinely superior retirement income vehicle. The new TPR code provides the governance scaffolding that interested employers and their advisers have been waiting for. With Royal Mail demonstrating proof of concept, the infrastructure for the next wave of UK CDC schemes is now in place.

Key takeaways

  • CDC pools longevity and investment risk across a collective membership, targeting stable retirement income without a DB guarantee
  • TPR's April 2026 code covers governance, actuarial certification, member communications, and wind-up planning
  • Annual actuarial certification is now mandatory, with the scheme actuary confirming the adjustment mechanism was correctly applied
  • Royal Mail's scheme provides the operational evidence base that shaped the 2026 code's practical requirements
  • CDC suits large employers with stable workforces and sustained contribution capacity; it is not a fit for all situations
  • The code paves the way for multi-employer and industry-wide CDC structures beyond the current single-employer model
