The December 2025 decision

The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee voted on 18 December 2025 to cut Bank Rate by 25 basis points to 3.75%. The decision was not unanimous — reflecting the MPC's continued caution in the face of services inflation that, while declining, remains above the 2% target. The accompanying statement acknowledged that the disinflation process was progressing broadly as expected, but signalled that further cuts would be data-dependent and gradual rather than automatic.

The December cut was the fourth 25bp reduction since the MPC began its easing cycle in August 2024, when it lowered Bank Rate from the 5.25% peak that had held since August 2023. From the vantage point of UK DB pension schemes, this rate cycle represents one of the most consequential monetary policy sequences in recent memory — the sharp rise from 0.1% in 2021 through to 5.25% transformed the funding landscape for DB schemes, and the subsequent easing is beginning, carefully, to reverse some of those gains.

The rate cycle in context: from 0.1% to 5.25% and back

To understand the significance of the current easing cycle, it helps to trace the full arc. Bank Rate stood at the historic low of 0.1% through the pandemic period, holding there until December 2021 when the MPC began its tightening cycle. Fourteen consecutive rate increases followed, culminating at 5.25% in August 2023 — the highest level since 2008. Throughout this period, gilt yields rose sharply in response, with 20-year nominal gilt yields moving from below 1% in early 2021 to above 4.5% by late 2023.

For DB pension schemes, the rise in gilt yields mechanically reduced the present value of future liability cash flows — since those liabilities are discounted at rates derived from gilts and high-quality corporate bonds. The aggregate improvement in scheme funding levels was substantial: the PPF 7800 index, which tracks the aggregate funding position of DB schemes, moved from deep deficit to aggregate surplus over this period. Schemes that locked in those improved funding levels through LDI hedging are now well-positioned. Those that did not will be watching the easing cycle more anxiously.

"Every 25bp cut in Bank Rate that transmits into lower long gilt yields adds roughly 2–4% to aggregate DB scheme liabilities in present value terms — a material headwind for schemes that have not locked in their funding improvements through liability-matching strategies."

Impact on gilt yields and DB liabilities

Bank Rate cuts do not translate directly or mechanically into equivalent movements in long-dated gilt yields. The relationship between short-term monetary policy and long-end yields is complex, and long gilt yields are also heavily influenced by global factors — particularly US Treasury yields, UK fiscal outlook, and inflation expectations. Nevertheless, a sustained easing cycle does tend to drag long yields lower over time, and the current cycle is no exception.

DB pension liabilities are discounted using the yields on gilts and investment-grade corporate bonds that best match the scheme's cash flow duration. As those yields fall, the present value of future benefit obligations rises — meaning the scheme's liabilities on a gilts basis increase. For a well-funded scheme with adequate LDI coverage, this liability increase is offset by rising gilt prices in the hedging portfolio. For schemes with partial hedging, or where hedging was reduced during the high-rate period, falling yields represent a genuine erosion of funding surplus.

LDI hedging and liability-matching

The LDI crisis of September 2022 — when a sudden spike in gilt yields triggered forced selling by leveraged LDI funds — prompted a sector-wide reassessment of hedging strategy, collateral buffers, and leverage ratios. Many schemes responded by reducing leverage and increasing the liquidity buffer within their LDI programmes. The consequence is that average hedge ratios in the sector are now somewhat lower than they were at the peak of the pre-2022 period, and schemes are exposed to more residual rate risk than they might have been.

With rates now falling, those schemes with higher residual rate exposure will see liabilities rise faster than their hedge portfolios offset. The appropriate response — increasing hedge ratios when gilt yields are at attractive levels — requires trustees to make a deliberate tactical decision. The December 2025 cut is a prompt to review whether current hedging levels remain appropriate for each scheme's funding level, journey plan, and risk tolerance.

LDI leverage and collateral buffers

Post-2022 reforms mean most LDI programmes carry larger collateral buffers and lower leverage. This reduces the acute liquidity risk exposed in 2022 — but it also reduces the degree of liability matching per unit of capital deployed. Trustees should confirm their effective hedge ratio at current yield levels.

What falling rates mean for buyout pricing

For schemes pursuing a full insurance buyout as their endgame, the rate environment matters in a different but equally material way. Insurers price bulk annuity transactions by reference to a portfolio of assets — primarily gilts and investment-grade credit — that they will hold to match the benefit liabilities being insured. When gilt yields are higher, insurers can offer more competitive pricing because the same premium buys a higher-yielding asset portfolio. As yields fall, buyout pricing tends to rise in relative terms — meaning schemes that transact later in the easing cycle will typically pay more than those who moved earlier.

The December 2025 cut reinforces the urgency for well-funded schemes that have been preparing for a buyout transaction to press ahead. Insurers remain busy — the pipeline of transactions entering the market has been substantial since 2023 — and schemes that delay risk both higher pricing and longer lead times as insurer capacity remains stretched. Schemes that are not yet at a funding level where buyout is realistic should focus on ensuring their assets are appropriately positioned to benefit from residual funding level improvements as the easing cycle progresses.

What schemes should review

The combination of falling Bank Rate, above-target inflation, and a maturing liability base means that December 2025 is a moment for active governance rather than passive monitoring. Trustees should review the scheme's current effective hedge ratio — not the nominal ratio set at the last investment review, but the actual sensitivity of funding level to a 25bp shift in gilt yields at current market levels. If that sensitivity is higher than the agreed risk budget permits, a hedging review is overdue.

Cash flow forecasts should also be updated to reflect current interest rate assumptions — both for the discount rate applied to liabilities and for the return expectations on the matching and growth asset portfolios. And for schemes approaching or already at their endgame funding target, the timing question for buyout should be revisited in the context of the current rate environment and insurer capacity outlook.

Key takeaways

  1. Bank Rate cut to 3.75% on 18 December 2025 — the fourth 25bp cut since August 2024, from a 5.25% peak.
  2. Falling yields increase DB liabilities in present value terms — schemes without full LDI hedging will see funding levels under pressure.
  3. Post-2022 LDI reform means lower leverage and larger buffers — but also lower effective hedge ratios for many schemes. Review yours.
  4. Buyout pricing will rise as yields fall — schemes ready for a transaction should engage insurers sooner rather than later in this cycle.
  5. An active governance review is warranted — check effective hedge ratio, update cash flow projections, and confirm endgame timing strategy.
