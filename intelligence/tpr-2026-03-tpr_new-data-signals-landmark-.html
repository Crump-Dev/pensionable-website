New data signals landmark shift from savings system to pensions system – but TPR warns smaller schemes must do more

05 March 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 6 min read

The structural change: what "landmark shift" actually means

For most of its 23-year history, auto-enrolment's central achievement has been measured in accumulation: how many people are saving, and how much. The system was designed around getting contributions in. The question of what happens when those contributions come out — and more precisely, how schemes support members through that transition — was deferred.

That deferral is ending. On 5 March 2026, TPR published its first-of-its-kind analysis of decumulation products within DC occupational pension schemes, based on 2025 DC scheme return data. The analysis signals what the regulator called a "landmark shift": for the first time, significant numbers of DC savers have access to retirement income products within their own scheme, without needing to exit to an insurance product or a retail drawdown platform. That is qualitatively different from what existed five years ago, and it reflects deliberate market development — primarily through the growth of master trusts — rather than regulatory mandate alone.

The Pension Schemes Bill, currently before Parliament, will make this structural shift permanent through the guided retirement duty: a new legislative requirement for schemes to provide default retirement solutions and improved support at the point members access their pensions. The TPR data published in March 2026 provides the baseline against which that duty will be enforced.

What the data shows

The TPR analysis — which excludes micro and hybrid DC schemes — reveals a market divided sharply along scale lines. Among the largest schemes (5,000+ members), 86% offer members at least one retirement income option. Among small schemes (12–99 members), that figure drops to 46%. Across all schemes, two fifths offer members nothing at the point of retirement — no drawdown, no annuity pathway, no guidance on what to do with a pot that may represent the entirety of their retirement savings.

The headline figure of 13.4 million members now able to access drawdown within their scheme is significant for a specific reason: drawdown was not historically available within occupational schemes. It was a retail product, accessible through individual pension contracts. The fact that it now exists at scale within the occupational market — largely through master trusts — represents a genuine structural development, not just a product addition. For members, it means the prospect of staying within a well-governed, low-cost institutional scheme through retirement rather than being pushed into the retail market at the point of maximum vulnerability.

The growth of master trusts is the mechanism behind this change. Their scale enables product development that is simply not viable for a small scheme with 50 members. A master trust with hundreds of thousands of members can build in-scheme drawdown, negotiate competitive annuity pathways, and invest in the member communication infrastructure needed to guide people through retirement decisions. A 75-member single employer scheme cannot — and should not be expected to — replicate that capability independently.

The decumulation challenge: what trustees actually need to deliver

Decumulation is harder than accumulation. In accumulation, the default works: most members do nothing, and auto-escalation and default investment strategies do the work for them. In decumulation, the member must make decisions — whether to take income, how much, in what form, and for how long — that are highly personal, highly consequential, and, in most cases, irreversible. TPR's own data from Nausicaa Delfas's March speech confirms that only one in five DC members has a plan for how to access their pension.

The Pension Schemes Bill's guided retirement duty is designed to address this by requiring default pathways — so that members who do nothing are guided into a reasonable retirement option, rather than leaving their pot untouched or making a poorly informed decision under pressure. But the duty will require schemes to have worked out what their default actually is, how it is structured, and how it meets the needs of their specific membership cohort.

"Too many members in smaller schemes are left without support when they reach retirement. This is not good enough. If you are not able to guide savers into the right retirement options for them, our message is clear: you should consider consolidation."

Joey Patel, Director of Policy, The Pensions Regulator — 5 March 2026

This is technically and commercially complex. Should the scheme pool longevity risk across members? When should it shift from growth-oriented to income-generating assets — and at what rate? Should it offer a blend of drawdown and annuity purchase? How does it account for members who have other income sources — a DB entitlement, property, or a partner's pension — against those for whom this DC pot is everything? These are not questions that can be answered with a single product. They require genuine design work, member data, and governance.

The gender pensions gap and decumulation design

Delfas noted in her March speech that the gender pensions gap stands at 48% — far exceeding the 7% gender pay gap. Women who take career breaks in their first decade of work face pot sizes around 16% lower at retirement. Default decumulation pathways that treat all members identically will systematically fail cohorts with materially different risk exposures and savings histories.

Why smaller schemes face the most pressure

The data makes the structural problem clear: small schemes cannot economically deliver what the guided retirement duty will require. TPR's Director of Policy Joey Patel stated it directly in the press release: "If you are not able to guide savers into the right retirement options for them, our message is clear: you should consider consolidation into a scheme that can offer value for money solutions."

This is a regulatory direction of travel, not merely a suggestion. The government's reform agenda — fewer, larger, better-governed schemes — is explicitly aimed at the long tail of small DC schemes that lack the scale to deliver member outcomes comparable to the master trust market. The guided retirement duty, once enacted, will create a compliance floor that many small schemes will not be able to meet without either consolidating or entering into commercial arrangements with providers that have the infrastructure in place.

The timing pressure is real. The Pension Schemes Bill is progressing, and trustees of smaller schemes need to start reviewing their decumulation offer now — before the duty comes into force — to understand the gap between what they currently provide and what will be required. That gap assessment is also a governance requirement in its own right: trustees have a fiduciary duty to act in members' best interests, and a scheme that offers no retirement income support is not fulfilling that duty.

Actions for trustees

Whether you are a trustee of a small single-employer scheme or a large master trust, the March 2026 data release and the guided retirement duty create a clear action list for the next 12 months. The question is not whether to engage with decumulation — the regulatory and legislative trajectory makes that engagement inevitable — but how to do it in a way that is proportionate to your scheme's scale and specific membership.

Key actions for trustees

  1. Audit your current decumulation offer. Document precisely what your scheme currently provides at the point a member reaches retirement — and what it does not. For schemes offering nothing, this is the starting point for understanding the gap relative to the guided retirement duty.
  2. Assess consolidation options honestly. For small schemes, the cost-benefit analysis of remaining independent versus consolidating into a master trust or multi-employer arrangement should be conducted with decumulation capability as a central criterion — not an afterthought.
  3. Understand your membership cohort. Age distribution, contribution history, gender split, and the proportion of members likely to have no other retirement income are all inputs to designing an appropriate default pathway. Generic solutions will not serve heterogeneous memberships well.
  4. Engage with administration and data systems now. Effective guided retirement requires high-quality member data — projected pot values, contribution histories, nominated beneficiaries, expression of wish information. Schemes that start the data remediation work now will be significantly better positioned when the duty comes into force.
