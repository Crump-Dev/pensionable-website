What the PPF 7800 Index Measures

The PPF 7800 Index measures the aggregate funding position of around 5,000 UK defined benefit (DB) schemes that are eligible for Pension Protection Fund cover. Published every month, it compares the total assets held by those schemes against their Section 179 liabilities — the estimated premium required to transfer PPF-level benefit obligations to an insurance company. It is distinct from technical provisions (the funding target used for statutory valuations) and from full buyout pricing, which is typically higher than Section 179 because it covers the full benefit rather than the PPF's reduced-benefit floor.

Month-to-month movements in the index reflect three main inputs: changes in long-dated gilt yields and corporate bond spreads (which drive the liability discount rate), changes in the asset values of schemes' investment portfolios, and movements in inflation expectations (which affect the cost of inflation-linked benefit increases). A falling liability value without a corresponding asset fall produces an improved funding ratio — which is precisely what happened across 2022 and much of 2023 as the Bank of England's rate hiking cycle played out.

The May 2026 Picture

May 2026 marks a meaningful rebound from the April softening: the aggregate funding ratio recovers to 131.4% — matching what will later prove to be the June figure — as total assets of £1,105.0bn sit against liabilities of £841.2bn, producing an aggregate surplus of £258.5bn. The sharp fall in total assets relative to April (from £1,161.3bn to £1,105.0bn) reflects scheme universe changes and liability-side movements rather than a straightforward asset loss — liabilities also contracted materially, from £887.6bn to £841.2bn, which is the key driver of the improved ratio.

The liability compression in May suggests that long-dated gilt yields firmed somewhat during the month, or that inflation expectations moderated, reducing the present value of future benefit cashflows across the universe. Either way, the funding ratio at 131.4% has now pushed back above the March level, confirming that the April moderation was transient. Schemes with strong LDI programmes will have captured most of this liability reduction in their hedging positions, while growth assets contributed to the broader asset picture.

"The ratio recovery to 131.4% in May, despite a fall in absolute asset values, demonstrates how liability movements can dominate the funding picture — and why LDI strategy remains central to preserving surplus."

Liability Matching and LDI in a Maturing Cycle

The May data reinforces a pattern that has defined UK DB pension management since the late 2010s: schemes that invested in robust liability-driven investment frameworks have captured the structural improvement in funding far more efficiently than those that relied primarily on growth assets. LDI involves holding assets — typically gilts, gilt repos, and interest rate swaps — whose value moves in line with the liability as yields change. For every basis point rise in long-dated yields, a fully hedged scheme sees its liability fall in concert with its hedge, leaving its net position unchanged. For an unhedged scheme, the same yield rise is a pure windfall — but the inverse is also true on the way down.

With the Bank of England having cut Bank Rate from a peak of 5.25% to 3.75% in gradual steps, the direction of rates is downward, even if the pace is measured. A further sustained fall in long-term yields would compress liabilities back upward, eroding surplus for any scheme with residual unhedged duration. The May data, with its lower absolute asset and liability totals, suggests that buyouts and scheme exits from the PPF-eligible universe are also reducing the pool — a sign that the endgame activity is real and structural, not merely a funding conversation.

Surplus context

May's £258.5bn surplus continues a remarkable multi-month run above £250bn — a threshold that would have seemed extraordinary even as recently as 2021. The total scheme universe size is also contracting as buyouts complete, which means each scheme remaining in the index is on average better funded than its predecessors.

Run-On as a Legitimate Strategy

The sustained surplus environment has prompted a genuine policy and governance debate about run-on: the option for a well-funded scheme to continue operating rather than pursue immediate buyout. The Pension Schemes Act 2021 created the statutory basis for trustees to run schemes open-endedly if members' interests are served, and regulatory guidance has evolved to accept run-on as a credible long-term strategy for schemes with strong governance, solvent sponsors, and an appropriate investment framework. For such schemes, run-on preserves optionality — the ability to take advantage of potential future improvements in buyout pricing, or to return surplus to the sponsor if legislation permits.

The counterargument is straightforward: buyout eliminates longevity risk, counterparty risk, and governance burden permanently. Most smaller schemes and those with weaker sponsor covenants will find the certainty of buyout more compelling than the theoretical upside of run-on. But for larger schemes — particularly those in the £500m-plus range — the run-on conversation is substantive. Trustees should ensure they have modelled both paths explicitly, with actuarial support, before defaulting to either option.

What Scheme Trustees Should Do Now

Key Actions

  1. Benchmark your funding ratio against the 131.4% aggregate. If your scheme sits below this level, understand the specific drivers — is it asset mix, unhedged liability exposure, or benefit structure?
  2. Model your run-on vs buyout economics with your actuary. The surplus environment has made this a genuine choice rather than a theoretical exercise; both paths need quantified analysis.
  3. Review your LDI programme's hedge ratio in light of the Bank of England's ongoing rate-cutting cycle. Residual duration exposure in a falling-rate environment works against you.
  4. Prepare a data remediation plan. If your scheme has GMP equalisation outstanding, pension increase exchange exercises, or missing deferred member addresses, these need resolving before any insurer transaction.
  5. Consider the Pensions Regulator's endgame guidance issued in 2025-2026 — TPR now expects trustees to maintain a documented, regularly reviewed endgame strategy aligned with scheme-specific circumstances.
