What the PPF 7800 Index Measures

The PPF 7800 Index is published monthly by the Pension Protection Fund, providing the definitive aggregate picture of funding health across approximately 5,000 UK defined benefit (DB) schemes under PPF protection. Each release compares total scheme assets against Section 179 liabilities — the estimated cost of transferring member benefit obligations to an insurer at the PPF's protected benefit level, rather than at full scheme rules. This makes the index a deliberately conservative measure: schemes typically carry full buyout liabilities that are higher than the Section 179 figure, which is why a scheme can be above 100% on the 7800 Index and still require additional funding to complete a buyout.

The index has become the most referenced barometer of UK DB pension health precisely because it is consistent, public, and monthly. Unlike triennial actuarial valuations — which give scheme-specific funding snapshots that are often 18 months out of date — the PPF 7800 Index responds in near-real time to movements in gilt yields, credit spreads, and inflation expectations. For policymakers, regulators, and advisers, it provides the clearest available read on whether the aggregate DB universe is moving toward or away from self-sufficiency.

The June 2026 Picture

The June 2026 release confirms an aggregate funding ratio of 131.4% — holding flat from May and marking the highest sustained level recorded over consecutive months in the history of the index. Full aggregate asset, liability, and surplus figures are not yet available at the time of publication and will be updated when the PPF releases its complete monthly dataset. The confirmation of 131.4% for a second consecutive month is itself significant: it suggests that neither asset returns nor discount rate movements made a material net impact on the aggregate position across May and June, implying relative stability in both gilt yields and investment portfolios.

The three-month view from March through June tells a coherent story. March opened at 131.0% with a £273.7bn surplus. April saw a dip to 130.8% as liabilities briefly expanded — likely on a modest yield compression — before rebounding sharply in May to 131.4% as liabilities contracted. June holds that level. Despite monthly noise in the underlying asset and liability totals, the ratio has moved in a tight corridor of 130.8% to 131.4% for the entire quarter. This stability reflects the effectiveness of LDI hedging across the majority of the scheme universe and suggests that most large schemes are now operating with funding positions that are relatively insulated from day-to-day market volatility.

"A 131.4% ratio held across two consecutive months signals something new: the UK DB system is not just well-funded — it is stable. The endgame is no longer a planning exercise. It is happening now."

What Drives the Funding Ratio

The June reading comes against a backdrop of a Bank of England that has cut Bank Rate from a 2023 peak of 5.25% to 3.75% in a measured sequence of reductions. Long-dated gilt yields, while not moving in lockstep with Bank Rate, have remained elevated by historical standards as the market prices in the UK's fiscal position and global term premium. The combination of historically high yields — suppressing liability present values — and strong investment performance over the preceding three years has driven the aggregate position to where it is today. LDI strategies, which caused significant market disruption in September 2022 when leveraged gilt pools faced margin calls, have since been restructured: lower leverage ratios and more liquid collateral buffers are now standard across most pooled LDI fund managers.

Inflation expectations are a secondary but meaningful variable. Gilt-linked benefit increases — particularly under legacy RPI-linked rules — add inflation sensitivity to scheme liabilities that persists even after interest rate hedging is in place. The difference between schemes with full inflation hedging and those without can be significant when breakeven inflation rates move by 20-30 basis points. In the current environment, with UK CPI gradually declining toward the Bank of England's 2% target but not yet consistently anchored there, inflation hedging remains a relevant consideration in maintaining funding stability.

Surplus context

While the full June surplus figure is pending, the consistency of the 131.4% ratio alongside May's confirmed £258.5bn surplus suggests the aggregate surplus has remained in the £250bn+ range — a level that would have been considered extraordinary just five years ago when the aggregate position briefly dipped below zero on a Section 179 basis in early 2020.

Endgame Options in a Surplus World

The sustained above-130% aggregate funding ratio has accelerated what the industry has long called the "endgame" — the final phase of a DB scheme's life as it transitions from active management toward a settled, low-risk position. Bulk annuity buyout remains the dominant destination: a transaction where an insurance company assumes full responsibility for member benefits in exchange for a premium. For schemes that are above their buyout funding level — not just their Section 179 level — this is now a live operational question rather than a long-term aspiration. Insurer appetite remains strong, competitive pricing is available for well-prepared schemes, and adviser pipelines are full.

But the sustained surplus has also opened space for more nuanced conversations. Schemes with strong sponsor covenants and sophisticated governance are increasingly examining run-on strategies: remaining open indefinitely, managing the scheme prudently, and potentially returning surplus to the employer under the rules introduced by recent pension legislation. The Pensions Regulator has signalled it is not opposed to run-on where the circumstances genuinely support it — provided trustees can demonstrate a clear framework, ongoing funding adequacy, and a credible pathway for members. The key regulatory expectation is that trustees have a documented, evidence-based endgame strategy, not just a default assumption that buyout will happen eventually.

What Scheme Trustees Should Do Now

Key Actions

  1. Confirm your own funding position on a buyout basis, not just Section 179. The gap between Section 179 and full buyout cost varies by scheme; understanding it is the first step in assessing transaction readiness.
  2. Formally document your endgame strategy with a target timeline. TPR's latest guidance is explicit: trustees should have a written strategy that is reviewed at least annually and shared with the sponsor.
  3. Assess insurer capacity and pricing through a broker-led market sounding exercise. The bulk annuity market can absorb significant volumes, but well-prepared schemes with clean data consistently achieve better pricing.
  4. Review LDI structure and collateral adequacy. Post-2022 regulatory expectations around leverage caps and liquidity buffers in pooled LDI funds are now embedded; confirm your scheme meets current best practice standards.
  5. Consider the member communication strategy. As schemes move toward buyout or run-on, members need timely, clear information — particularly around any changes to who is responsible for their benefits and what protections apply.
