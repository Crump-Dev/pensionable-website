TPR urges vigilance after rise in impersonation fraud against pension savers

11 March 2026 — The Pensions Regulator 5 min read

The Pensions Regulator has issued a fresh warning to trustees and scheme administrators following a sustained rise in impersonation fraud targeting pension savers across the UK. The regulator's guidance reflects a shift in the fraud landscape: criminals are no longer relying on crude cold-calling tactics alone. Instead, they are deploying increasingly convincing imitation techniques — spoofed official identities, AI-generated correspondence, and cloned websites — to extract member data and authorise fraudulent transfers.

The warning matters because impersonation fraud sits at the most difficult end of the scam spectrum to detect. Unlike obvious high-pressure cold calls, these attacks are designed to appear entirely routine. A member receiving what looks like an official letter from TPR, or a call appearing to come from their scheme administrator's number, has very little reason to be suspicious — unless the scheme has explicitly warned them what to look for.

The Rise in Impersonation Fraud

Impersonation fraud in the pensions context typically involves criminals posing as one of three trusted sources: The Pensions Regulator itself, HMRC, or the member's own scheme administrator. Each identity is chosen deliberately. TPR and HMRC carry inherent authority — a letter or call purportedly from either organisation commands attention and compliance. Scheme administrators carry familiarity: members may have spoken to them before and have no reason to doubt a follow-up contact.

What makes the current wave particularly concerning is the quality of the deception. Advances in generative AI have made it straightforward to produce correspondence that matches the tone, layout, and visual identity of official communications. Caller ID spoofing technology — freely available and inexpensive — allows criminals to display any number they choose. The result is that the traditional advice to "check who's calling" provides far weaker protection than it once did.

Action Fraud data has consistently shown pension fraud as one of the highest-value fraud categories per victim. Average losses from pension scams — across all types — have been recorded above £50,000 per case, with some victims losing their entire retirement savings. The FCA and TPR's joint ScamSmart campaign has flagged impersonation as a fast-growing sub-category within that already serious picture.

Scale of the problem

Action Fraud reports average pension scam losses in excess of £50,000 per victim. The FCA and TPR's ScamSmart data shows impersonation fraud is growing as a proportion of total pension fraud reports, driven by cheaper and more accessible spoofing and AI tools.

How Impersonation Attacks Work

Understanding the mechanics of a typical attack is the first step in building effective defences. Impersonation fraud against pension savers generally follows a recognisable pattern, even if the specific details vary.

Initial contact. The member receives unsolicited contact — most commonly a phone call, but increasingly also email or even postal correspondence. The caller or sender presents as a known, authoritative body: TPR conducting a "scheme review," HMRC notifying the member of an unpaid tax liability relating to their pension, or the scheme administrator flagging an "urgent account matter."

Establishing credibility. The fraudster deploys information designed to build trust. This may include the member's name, date of birth, scheme name, or employer — details that are often available through data breaches, social media, or purchased lists. The use of specific personal detail makes the contact feel legitimate. Caller ID spoofing reinforces the impression that the call is coming from a real, known source.

Manufactured urgency. Once credibility is established, urgency is introduced. Common pretexts include a deadline to avoid a tax penalty, a limited-time opportunity to "protect" funds before a regulatory change, or an allegation that the member's pension is at risk and must be transferred to a "safe" holding account immediately. The urgency is designed to override the member's natural caution and prevent them from pausing to verify the contact independently.

The ask. The fraudster then seeks either a transfer of funds to a new account — often described as a "temporary holding vehicle" or a "government-backed scheme" — or personal authentication data such as PINs, security answers, or National Insurance numbers that allow them to make requests through the scheme's own systems.

"The sophistication of modern pension impersonation fraud means that checking who called you is no longer sufficient. Schemes must assume that members cannot reliably distinguish a fraudulent contact from a genuine one — and build their processes accordingly."

The Pensions Regulator, March 2026

What TPR Is Asking Schemes to Do

TPR's guidance sets out specific expectations for how schemes should respond to the rise in impersonation fraud. These are not optional good-practice recommendations — they reflect TPR's view of what a well-governed scheme looks like in the current threat environment.

Member verification procedures. Schemes should have documented, consistent procedures for verifying member identity across all channels. Telephone verification should not rely on information that a fraudster could reasonably obtain. Schemes should use layered authentication — combining knowledge factors (information only the true member would know) with out-of-band verification (a call-back to a number held on record, not a number provided by the caller).

Call-back protocols. When a member contacts the scheme by phone requesting any material change — a transfer, address update, or bank account change — staff should be trained to end the call and contact the member back on a number drawn from the scheme's own records. The call-back number must be the one held on file, not one provided by the caller in the same conversation. This single procedural step defeats the majority of impersonation attempts.

Staff training and escalation. Front-line administration staff are the primary point of contact for most member interactions. TPR expects schemes to provide regular, updated training on current fraud typologies and to maintain clear escalation paths for contacts that display warning signs. Staff should feel empowered to pause or refuse a request without fear of complaint, supported by documented policy.

The Red Flags to Communicate to Members

Beyond internal process controls, TPR is asking schemes to actively communicate scam red flags to members — particularly at moments when members are most likely to be targeted, such as approaching retirement or during a transfer process. The following indicators should be clearly and regularly communicated:

  • Unsolicited contact from anyone claiming to represent TPR, HMRC, or the scheme itself — TPR does not contact individual members directly to discuss their pension savings.
  • Pressure to act quickly or claims that a time-limited opportunity or penalty deadline applies — legitimate organisations do not create artificial urgency around pension decisions.
  • Guaranteed or unusually high investment returns — no legitimate pension investment can guarantee returns, and any such promise should be treated as a scam indicator.
  • Requests to transfer to an unfamiliar provider, particularly one described as a "government-backed" or "protected" scheme — legitimate safe harbour accounts do not exist in the way scammers describe them.
  • Requests for PINs, passwords, or one-time codes — no legitimate scheme administrator or regulator will ever ask a member for their authentication credentials.
  • Instructions not to discuss the contact with family or advisers — this is a reliable hallmark of fraudulent intent.

5 actions for scheme administrators

  1. Audit your verification procedures now. Map every channel through which a member can request a material change and confirm that each one has a documented, tested authentication procedure that does not rely on caller-supplied information.
  2. Implement mandatory call-back on all sensitive requests. Transfer requests, bank account changes, and address updates should trigger an automatic call-back to the number held on file — not the number provided by the caller.
  3. Train staff on current fraud typologies at least annually. Ensure training covers impersonation fraud specifically, including the use of AI-generated communications and spoofed caller IDs, and document attendance.
  4. Add scam warning communications to all transfer packs. Every member receiving transfer documentation should also receive clear, jargon-free guidance on the red flags that indicate their transfer may have been initiated under fraudulent influence.
  5. Establish an escalation path and log all suspicious contacts. Staff should know exactly who to contact when a call or request displays warning signs, and all such contacts should be logged to support pattern detection and, if necessary, regulatory reporting.

TPR's message is clear: the burden of protection cannot be left to members alone. Pension savers — particularly those approaching retirement who may have little recent experience of the scheme's normal communication patterns — are inherently vulnerable to well-crafted impersonation. Schemes that treat scam prevention as a member responsibility rather than a scheme responsibility are out of step with regulatory expectations.

The investment in robust verification procedures, staff training, and proactive member communication is modest compared to the reputational, regulatory, and human cost of a successful impersonation attack against one of the scheme's members.
